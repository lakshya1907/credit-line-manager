# Analytics Findings

Generated by `python run_analytics.py` against the models trained by the latest `python run_all.py` run. Raw output: `data/processed/analytics/*.csv` (gitignored — regenerate locally). This document is the write-up; the CSVs are the evidence.

## 1. A real bug found while building this analysis

The policy-comparison exercise below (§3) initially produced a nonsensical result: halving `EAD_BUDGET` cut approved increases from 4,610 to 2,288, but `total_ep_uplift` stayed **bit-for-bit identical** at 46,216,462.90. That's the kind of thing that should never happen — cutting the budget in half obviously has *some* profit cost — and it was the tell that something in the reporting was wrong, not the model.

Root cause, in [`src/portfolio_opt.py`](../src/portfolio_opt.py): when `portfolio_select` rejects an increase for exceeding budget, it reverted `action` back to `"hold"` and `recommended_limit` back to `current_limit`, but left `ep_uplift`, `pd_recommended`, `ead_recommended`, `el_uplift_proxy`, and `ead_uplift` at their **pre-rejection** (would-have-been-approved) values. So a row now labeled `"hold"` still reported the profit of the increase that was never actually approved, and `total_ep_uplift = out["ep_uplift"].sum()` silently summed in the hypothetical profit of every rejected increase alongside the real profit of approved ones.

Fixed (all "recommended" columns now revert to "current" on rejection, matching a genuine hold). Verified the fix by temporarily reverting it and confirming the test suite's two regression tests for this (`tests/test_portfolio_opt.py::test_increase_rejected_when_it_would_exceed_budget`, `test_summary_total_ep_uplift_excludes_rejected_increases`) both fail against the buggy version and pass against the fix. Every `total_ep_uplift` figure below is post-fix.

## 2. Segment breakdowns

Full detail: `data/processed/analytics/segment_summary.csv`.

**Behavioral segments track risk sensibly.** `delinquency_tier` shows the guardrails doing their job: `severe(3mo+)` delinquency gets a 0% increase rate (1,193 customers, none approved for an increase), `mild(1-2mo)` gets 1.5%, `none` gets 25.9%. `util_tier` is more interesting: `low(<30%)` utilization customers get decreased 81.8% of the time and average only $147.87 EP uplift each, far below `medium` ($1,694) and `high` ($4,317). That's not a bug — a customer using under 30% of a high limit has little exposure to earn revenue on either way, so the profit-maximizing move is trimming the unused limit (reducing idle exposure) rather than raising it further; there's nothing to lend against.

**Fair-lending flag.** `SEX`, `EDUCATION`, and `MARRIAGE` are fed to `pd_model` as raw numeric features today (`build_features` passes them through unchanged, `pd_model.py` trains on them directly) — in the US, ECOA prohibits using sex or marital status as a factor in a credit decision, independent of whether it happens to be predictive here. `disparate_approval_ratio()` (the "four-fifths rule" ratio fair-lending review commonly uses as a trigger for further review, not a legal determination on its own) came out:

| segment | min/max increase-rate ratio | flag |
|---|---|---|
| `sex_segment` | 0.860 | ok |
| `marriage_segment` | 0.683 | **REVIEW** |

`marriage_segment`'s low ratio is between `"other"` (15.2% increase rate, n=323) and `"unknown"` (22.2%, n=54) — both small groups, so treat this as "worth a closer look," not proof of discrimination; but the underlying issue (protected characteristics as raw model inputs) is real and worth fixing regardless of what any single ratio says on a given run. This is a modeling recommendation, not something this analytics pass fixes.

## 3. Cutoff-based PD "backtest"

Why not a classic time-based train/test split: this dataset is one cross-sectional snapshot per customer (6 months of behavior, one future default outcome) — there's no per-customer signup date and no repeated observations at different points in time, so "train before date T, test after" isn't meaningful here (see `src/analytics/backtest.py`'s module docstring). What the data *does* support: how much predictive signal comes from *how much history* you have.

`restricted_feature_backtest` trains a PD model on a simplified, consistent feature set (utilization, payment ratio, delinquency, bill level) built from only the N most recent months, for N = 6, 3, 1:

| window (months) | val ROC-AUC | val PR-AUC |
|---|---|---|
| 6 | 0.7686 | 0.5352 |
| 3 | 0.7702 | 0.5337 |
| 1 | 0.7607 | 0.5329 |

(Absolute values aren't comparable to the production `pd_model`'s reported metrics — this uses a smaller, deliberately identical-across-windows feature set so the comparison isolates window length, not feature engineering differences.)

**Finding: recency matters more than history length.** Going from 6 months down to 3 months of history costs essentially nothing (ROC-AUC is actually marginally *higher* at 3 months, within noise). Going down to 1 month costs about 1 point of ROC-AUC. In other words, months 4–6 add close to zero marginal predictive value on top of the most recent 3 months — practically relevant if this were ever extended to score newer customers with thinner history: 3 months of behavior appears to be enough to capture most of what 6 months buys you.

## 4. Policy comparison

Full detail: `data/processed/analytics/policy_comparison.csv`. Now cheap (~0.3s per policy) thanks to the decision-engine vectorization — each row below is a full 30k-customer re-run under a different guardrail/budget.

| policy | `PD_INCREASE_MAX` | `EAD_BUDGET` | approved increases | EAD used | total EP uplift |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| current | 0.08 | 50M | 4,610 | 50.0M (100%) | 42.93M |
| tighter_pd_guardrail | 0.04 | 50M | 905 (−80%) | 10.4M | 32.52M (−24%) |
| looser_pd_guardrail | 0.16 | 50M | 5,218 (+13%) | 50.0M (100%) | 36.85M (−14%) |
| smaller_ead_budget | 0.08 | 25M | 2,288 (−50%) | 25.0M (100%) | 30.97M (−28%) |
| larger_ead_budget | 0.08 | 100M | 5,291 (+15%) | 53.2M | 46.22M (+8%) |

Two things worth flagging:

- **`EAD_BUDGET` is the binding constraint, not `EL_BUDGET`.** Every scenario except `tighter_pd_guardrail` saturates its EAD budget at exactly 100%; `used_el` never comes close to `el_budget` in any of them (consistent with the known `EL_BUDGET`-never-binds modeling limitation documented in `CLAUDE.md`). Any future EAD-vs-EL budget tuning should treat EAD as the lever that actually matters today.
- **Looser guardrails ≠ more profit.** `looser_pd_guardrail` approves *more* increases (+13%) than `current` but total profit *drops* 14%. Loosening `PD_INCREASE_MAX` doesn't just add more approvals on top of the existing plan — it changes which action is optimal for many customers *before* the portfolio budget filter even runs (a customer previously guardrailed into "hold" or "decrease" can now be evaluated as "increase"), and under the same EAD budget the greedy ROI-sorted selection has to spread that budget across more, lower-quality candidates. More approvals with a fixed budget can mean lower total profit, not higher — worth keeping in mind before treating "approval count" as a proxy for "better policy."
- **Diminishing returns on budget.** Doubling `EAD_BUDGET` (50M → 100M) only lifts profit 8% and approvals 15% — most of the high-ROI opportunities are already being captured at the current budget; the marginal customer let in by a bigger budget is a lower-ROI one by construction (the greedy selection already took the best ones first).

## 5. What this doesn't cover

- The counterfactual PD's correlation-vs-causation issue (documented in `CLAUDE.md`) isn't addressed here — it's why `EL_BUDGET` never binds in any of these scenarios, and it would need a modeling fix (e.g. damping how much PD is allowed to move from a limit change alone), not an analytics one.
- The fair-lending flag above is a screening signal, not a compliance review — treating `disparate_approval_ratio` as a pass/fail gate would be a mistake; the real fix is not training the PD model on protected characteristics in the first place.
